What happens on the day

The donation process, step by step

  1. 1

    Register & health history

    A short, private interview about your health, medicines and recent travel. Bring a photo ID.

  2. 2

    Mini check-up

    Staff check your haemoglobin, pulse, blood pressure and temperature to make sure donating is safe for you.

  3. 3

    The donation

    You lie back on a cot. A sterile, single-use needle collects about 350–450 ml of blood in 8–10 minutes. It feels like a quick pinch.

  4. 4

    Rest & refreshments

    Relax for 10–15 minutes with a drink and a snack, then carry on with your day. Drink extra fluids and skip heavy exercise until tomorrow.

Quick check: can I donate today?

Answer six questions. Nothing is saved or sent anywhere.

Are you between 18 and 65 years old?
Do you weigh at least 45 kg?
Are you feeling well today — no fever, cold or flu?
Has it been at least 3 months (men) or 4 months (women) since you last gave blood?
No major surgery, blood transfusion, tattoo or piercing in the last 12 months?
Not pregnant, breastfeeding, or given birth in the last 12 months?

When to wait before donating

Until you feel better

Cold, flu, sore throat, headache or general weakness.

6 months

Minor surgery, tooth extraction or other dental surgery.

12 months

Major surgery, receiving a blood transfusion, typhoid, or an endoscopy. Many blood banks also defer for 12 months after a tattoo or piercing.

Women

Not during menstruation, pregnancy or breastfeeding; wait 12 months after delivery and 6 months after an abortion.

Not eligible

Heart disease or a past heart attack, epilepsy, hepatitis, HIV, and some chronic conditions such as uncontrolled diabetes. Ask the blood bank's doctor if you're unsure.

Why donate

Blood can't be made in a factory

Demand is higher than supply

Accident victims, mothers in childbirth, children with thalassaemia, cancer and surgery patients all depend on donated blood. A 2019 study in The Lancet Haematology estimated that India had one of the largest blood shortfalls in the world. Regular voluntary donors are the most reliable way to close that gap.

It's safe

Every needle and bag is sterile and used once. You can't catch an infection from donating.

Your body refills fast

Fluid volume returns within a day or two; red cells are fully replaced over the following weeks.

It ends panic searches

When families scramble for "replacement donors" in an emergency, precious hours are lost. Registered voluntary donors fix that.

Myths vs facts

"Donating makes you weak."

Healthy adults feel normal within hours. Eat, drink fluids and rest briefly — that's all.

"Vegetarians can't donate."

Diet doesn't matter — what matters is your haemoglobin, which is checked before you donate.

"It takes the whole day."

The donation takes about 10 minutes; the full visit is usually under an hour.
